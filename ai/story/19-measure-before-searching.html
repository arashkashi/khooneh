Measure before searching

The owner asked whether optimisation could find answers faster. It could, but the first runs showed the danger. A search spent minutes trying shapes for the sunken court, and every good shape got the same sun: the building behind decides the light, not the court. The owner drew the lesson. Make sure an optimisation is meaningful before running it, and start coarse.

Decision. Every optimisation idea now passes three gates. A pre-check tries only the extremes and asks whether any result can move by an amount a person would notice: half an hour of sun, a point of sky, a square metre of floor. If not, it stops and says so. A coarse screen then finds which dimensions matter. Only those get a short search. Every tool is written as reusable functions, and every result is saved with the commit it came from.

On iteration 4 the gates did their work in seconds. The court stopped at the pre-check: size it for the yard you want, because its light won't change. Of the balconies, only one mattered. The fourth-floor balcony over the small duplex's bedroom takes that room from 14.5% of the sky to 1.3%, and from 3.4 to 1.8 hours of December sun. The run also caught a drawing slip: the birch stands 0.35 m too close to the balcony above it.

One level up, a stacking study asked which home should take which part of each floor. Of 48 ways to give the four homes their floors, only iteration 4's meets the area bands. Of thousands of finer splits, 20 work, and iteration 4 sits among the best. The one change worth making is the same balcony again. Without it, and with the small duplex's living room moved up to the third floor, that home gains about two hours of winter sun. The big home would lose its sunniest drying line, so the owner decides.

What it means. Two independent tools, one tuning dimensions and one sharing floors, pointed at the same balcony. That is the kind of agreement worth trusting. Most other knobs on this plot are held by the neighbours, and saying so plainly is itself a result.

Meanwhile the owner settled the court tree. Over the day it went from poplar to ironwood and walnut, and finally to a silver birch in its upright form. It is white-trunked and straight, and it fits the court without crowding the balconies, though its crown stays below the sunny zone. A first walkthrough was rendered from the 1399 model: a living room with a carpet and floor cushions, an L-shaped kitchen, and late October sun through the yard window. And the refinement page broke for a moment, because the browser still remembered home "H" from a design that no longer had one. It now forgets what no longer exists and keeps the owner's notes.

Late that evening the owner turned to the windows: smaller on the north, perhaps bigger on the south. On this plot the "north" is the street side, facing north-east, and the "south" is the yard, facing south-west. Measuring first, as the day had taught, showed where size matters. Only the fourth floor's yard glass buys winter sun; everywhere else bigger glass buys daylight and costs heat. So the street got three columns of narrow, upright windows in deep brick reveals, the same on every floor. The upper floors lost about a third of their street glass; the first and second kept nearly all of theirs, because the building across the alley already takes their sky. The yard got tall windows from a low sill nearly to the ceiling, and hinged timber louvred shutters against the late-summer afternoon sun and the eyes of the building 6 m behind. The small duplex's bedroom kept its windows, since more glass there would only lose heat.

Decision. Street windows are narrow, upright and aligned in three columns; yard windows are tall, with a fixed satin lower pane as the guard and hinged louvred shutters. All windows are hinged. Each window's sill, head and type are now data, and the sun tool reads them (design/research/windows-1405.md).
