The plan becomes ordinary

The owner looked at iteration 2 on the workbench and wasn't impressed. The faults were small and many. There were stores inside stores, and doors that couldn't swing beside a column. Bedrooms were sized as doubles for one person. On two floors a guest walked past the family's doors to reach the living room. They asked for common, intuitive things, not new ideas.

So their remarks became rules first. There is at most one store per home, with shelves behind a curtain. Doors are hinged only, and every bath door opens outward. Each home has one couple room, and every other bedroom is for one person, about four times the bed. Two respected designs were measured, Ozgol in Tehran and Cité Manifeste. They showed what open means in numbers: their largest open space is about two thirds of the home, and halls are only a few per cent of it.

Decision. Iteration 3 is a plain apartment plan. An entry hall opens at the door. The living room and kitchen sit together on the yard, and a family hall leads to the bedrooms and bath. The west columns move into the side wall. Iteration 2 is kept for comparison.

Every hard rule now holds. Door flags fell from 25 to 2 and furniture gaps from 12 to 1, partly because the measures themselves became fairer. The parents' and second homes now look like the reference designs: about 60% of each is one open space. The big home is still hall-heavy, because its private stair takes floor on two levels.

What it means. Legible before clever. Open space is what lets people adapt a home. Rooms are sized to their use, construction is simple, and the order runs from guest to family. Each of these is now written down as a rule or a measure, so the next design can be checked against them and not just admired.

One thing the owner missed: the basement's link to the sunken court and its little garden. The doors are still there. But on the plan they read as two doors in a wall, not as a connection. Making that link real is the first task for iteration 4.
