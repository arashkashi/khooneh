The rooms become numbers

The form asked what the building should be. Now the owner wanted to get inside the current design and push on it: how doors open, what they hide, where the futons go, whether a tenant can bring beds. They gave a space to explore, not a list: minimal rooms, carpets and sleeping on the floor, tables for work, beds because tenants may want them, and movement and sight turned into numbers.

So the design got its own page, the workbench. It draws walls, doors, lockers and furniture on the plan, and lets the owner move the weights of an optimiser. Rooms are not moved. Everything added is marked as the AI's proposal.

Decision. The plan stays as authored. Hitting concrete or another door is never a matter of weights. Anything that would change a room is offered only as a marked suggestion.

Drawing real doors found things the room checks couldn't. Pantry doors stand half behind a column. The bedrooms' second way out on the third floor was a door 0.3 m wide, which the graph had counted as a door. In three homes the front door looks straight at the bath door. On the parents' and second floors a guest walks past the family's doors to reach the living room, the reverse of the old order of biruni and andaruni.

What the optimiser showed. It clears every door collision and about half the clearance points. It barely moves privacy and doesn't touch furniture fit. Those belong to the plan, and no hinge will repair them. That is the most useful thing it said.

Then the owner asked for the size of every space and for a link to each one, full-page, large enough to read. So every floor, home and room now has its own address. Partway through, they also said they would open the page themselves, so it has been run only through tests and a stand-in for the browser. Nobody has yet looked at how it reads on a screen.
