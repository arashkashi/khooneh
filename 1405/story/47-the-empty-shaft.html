47. The empty shaft

18 November 2026

Beside the lift there was a box on every floor, 1.5 by 2.1 metres, from the basement to the top floor, with a clear patch kept on the roof above it. On the plans it was called an air shaft for night cooling, "a closet until needed". You asked whether it really works, and if not, to finish it or take it out, with the numbers first.

What it was

It was empty. Nothing opened into it on any floor: no vent, no door, no window. The roof slab closed its top. It could not even be used as a closet, because no door could reach it: it stood between the lift's concrete wall, the wall to the neighbour, a pipe shaft and another concrete wall that holds the building in an earthquake. Together it took about 19 m² of floor and 3 m² of the roof garden, and nothing in the design counted on it. A small pipe box in the basement, which the plans listed beside it, turned out to be something else: the pipes of the basement's shower room.

Would it cool the homes?

Qazvin's summer nights are cool, about 18 °C, but calm. A finished shaft would draw warm air out of the homes on still nights. The numbers say it would help little. A, B and the caretaker's home already have windows on the street and on the yard, so air runs through them. The young couple's home (D) faces the yard only, but it has two floors and a spiral stair, so warm air rises and leaves through the bedroom window. The shaft would make the next day at most about half a degree cooler in A and D, and nothing in B or the caretaker's home. On the top floor the air would even run the wrong way, from the shaft into B's living room, carrying the smell of the kitchens below.

And in winter?

Shut for the winter, a shaft is still a chimney. Warm air leaks out through its closed vents and its roof cap, and real dampers leak. Without dampers it would lose 16 kW on a cold night, as much as heating three homes. With ordinary dampers it would still lose 2 to 4 kW. Even with the best dampers, insulation and a sealed cap, D would lose a tenth more heat, and A a little more than the new rule allows. The new rule says a closed shaft may add at most 3% to any home's winter heat loss. Even empty, the old box already cost D about 7%, because only bare concrete stood between D's kitchen and the cold box.

What was decided

You asked us to decide this one for you. The answer is to remove it. A finished shaft would join three homes, a path for smoke in a fire, for smells, and for winter cold, and the cooling it gives is small. So each floor's piece is now closed at its floor and ceiling, a sealed box, with insulation toward the home beside it. D's extra winter loss falls to about 1.5%. The roof gets its corner back: a place for a tool chest against the lift wall and room to turn the trolley.

The homes do not gain floor yet. To make the box a cupboard, a door would have to go through one of the concrete walls that hold the building in an earthquake, and the building is already short of those walls across the plot. If the engineer allows a door there, each box can become a cupboard of 2.8 m² for the home beside it: A's kitchen, D's kitchen and shower room, and B's living room.

For summer nights the homes rely on their windows on both sides, D's own stair, and ceiling fans, which make a warm evening feel about two degrees cooler.
