The story

92. A curtain, a panel and a cupboard

22 November 2026

On 22 November the owner asked for every open card to be settled "to the best of your knowledge and objectively correct". The owner had already handed these choices to the architect, on 21 November. This chapter covers five of those cards: four in the small duplex D and the big home B, and one rule for every door in the building. Each card was measured before choosing, and in three of them the measuring changed the answer the card had first recommended.

D's loft door becomes a curtain

The door between D's upper entry and its open bedroom floor had a column 0.15 m into its opening. Its 0.85 m leaf left 0.66 m clear, under the 0.70 m every door must give, and no hinged leaf fits between the party wall and the column. The opening now takes the whole 0.75 m between them and closes with a full-width linen curtain on a ceiling track. The track runs on over the wall beside the opening, so the drawn-back curtain parks there and the way keeps all 0.75 m.

When the curtain is drawn, it hides the bed from the upper entry, as the door did. Hinged doors stay the rule: a curtain is not a door, and the owner likes curtains over small spaces. The door rules now measure a curtain's opening too, so this one passes on its width, not by being a curtain. Goals rose from 71.5 to 77.0, because D's homes goal had been capped while this door failed.

The guard by the bed, as it really is

The card proposed a solid panel along the guard's 1.8 m that faces the bed. Measuring showed that only part of that edge is guard. The spiral turns 490°, so its last tread lands at the opening's south-west, toward the bed. The west 1.0 m of that edge is where you step off the stair. Only the east 0.85 m is guard.

That guard is 1.1 m high, with bars under 0.1 m apart, so the fall rules are already met. What a bed 0.8 m away adds is different:

  • the open stairwell is the first thing seen from the pillow;
  • small things can roll through the bars;
  • whoever gets up at night needs to find the top tread in the dark.

So the 0.85 m of guard becomes solid to 0.9 m, with the rail above it. A low step light, 0.3 m up on the guard's end post at the stair head, lights the top tread and the floor to the bed. A new tunable rule asks the same of any bed within 1.2 m of an opening's guard. Turning the bed was no answer: the east wall it would stand against is 0.6 m long.

D's mop comes downstairs

D kept its mop, vacuum and ironing board upstairs, over the spiral from the day floor where they are used. A bucket of water does not belong on a spiral's narrow inner treads. The card proposed a 0.55 × 0.40 m cupboard in the guest WC's south-east corner. At that size it would have stood 0.05 m in the WC door's opening and in the basin's front space.

What fits is 0.5 × 0.3 m, between the door's jamb and the south wall, 2.4 m tall. Its doors carry a child latch, the cleaners go on its top shelves, the vacuum charges inside, and the bucket is filled and emptied at the WC's floor drain.

The first try moved the basin 0.35 m north on the west wall. The dead-floor rule caught a 0.17 m² corner left behind it, and the towel hooks went onto the cupboard's side, where no wall is. So the basin turns onto the south wall beside the cupboard, with its mirror and the mirror's light, and both open onto the same floor. The WC keeps every clear space its rules ask. D gives 0.15 m² of WC floor.

B's raised bay: no windows

From the drone the bay over B's living room reads as a plant room. The card's answer was a band of high windows on its south face. The measurements say the room does not need them:

  • B's living room already has 11.4 m² of yard glass for 31 m² of floor;
  • it sees 35 to 40% of the sky;
  • it has about eight hours of sun in every season.

That south face looks south-west. High glass there would bring summer afternoon sun into a 5.5 m room, out of reach for a blind, and it would lose heat in a Qazvin winter. Windows on the garden's sides would let the shared roof look down into B's living room.

So the bay gets no windows. The faces the garden sees, north and west, carry the garden's own 50%-open timber screen, 0.1 m off the brick. Three people still have to decide before it is built:

  • the permit office, on the bay itself; if the bay is refused, the screen goes with it;
  • the structural engineer, on the screen's fixings and wind load;
  • the fire officer, on timber on a roof.

0.70 m stays the floor for doors

Seventeen doors give 0.72 m clear: eight in B, three in C, three in D and three shared, among them the −1 landing's door. Making 0.75 m a hard floor would fail all of them. It would not take 0.90 m leaves, as the card thought: 0.85 m leaves give 0.77 m, and every one of those walls has room. The real cost is the floor each wider leaf sweeps, about 0.07 m² a door, and halls that are already tight in D.

The step-free home A has 0.82 m or more at every door, and so does every front door. So 0.70 m stays the floor, and 0.75 m stays a target that the pages flag. The landing's door belongs to the shared floors and is only reported here: its wall has room for a wider leaf.
