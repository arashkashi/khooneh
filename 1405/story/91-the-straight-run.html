The story

91. The straight run

22 November 2026

The owner asked the architect to settle the open cards "to the best of your knowledge and objectively correct". Five of them belong to the first floor's step-free home A and to the caretaker's home C, with D's fridge alongside. Each was measured again before it was judged. One door moved, two fridges got housings, and three cards were left as they are, each for a measured reason. No home gains or loses any floor.

A's front door, square to the lift

The lift's car is 1100 × 1400 mm, too small for a wheelchair to turn in. A chair therefore turns in the ground floor's 1.65 m hall, rides up backwards and comes out forwards. On the first floor it then needs a straight run, not a big landing. A's front door shared only 0.46 m with the lift's door across the 1.05 m landing. It now moves 0.45 m along the landing's light wall, and the two openings share 0.86 m. The card said "north", but y grows southward on the plan, so the move is south, toward the lift's middle. The chair turns inside, in A's 1.75 m entry hall. A 1.5 × 1.5 m landing on every floor would have taken 2.7 m² from A, B and C and served no one the straight run does not.

A new hard rule, LIFT-landing, keeps this. On the step-free home's floor, the front door's clear opening must share at least 0.80 m with the lift's door, or the landing must hold a 1.5 m turning disc; and the room behind the door must hold that disc.

Three cards kept as they are

  • A's living-room sill stays at 0.90 m. The UK guidance behind the card counts where the glass starts. A 0.85 m sill starts its glass at about 0.92 m, behind the frame's 70 mm rail, so it would satisfy a sill check without giving the view. Rule WIN-sill now measures the glass line (parameter frame_m). A 0.6 m sill would give a real low view. But in A and C the floor cushions stand along the yard glass, so the low pane would sit behind people's backs and heads: cold, overlooked by the building 6 m behind, no extra daylight, and a transom at a floor-sitter's eye. The soft check still flags A, and the card says so.
  • A's kitchen window stays at 1.70 m. Widening it to 2.05 m lifts the daylight factor from 0.67 to 0.78 %. That gain is under the 0.3 points the project counts as worthwhile, and it costs 35 kWh a year, a permit drawing and the window's line with D's kitchen above.
  • C's kitchen stays an inner kitchen. With the building behind, a worktop on C's floor sees sky only within 1.50 m of the yard glass. C's runs are 4.6-7.0 m back. A wider window cannot change that; it only lifts the living room from 1.60 to 1.74 %. Moving the sink and hob to the yard wall would reach the sky, but it would take about 7 m² of the living room. It would also split the window, leave the hall's twin fridge 7 m from the sink and stretch the sink's drain to about 5 m.

Fridges that read as joinery

D's fridge-freezer and C's twin now stand in housings. Light-oak panels sit flush with each front, with a cupboard over the appliance up to 2.40 m, under the halls' cooling bulkheads. C's twin moved 2 cm in, inside its own place, so both its panels stand in its own home. D's fridge could not move: 2 cm east would block the drawers in the run beside it. So its panel on that side stops at the run's front line, and its side to the dining room stays bare. A side that a wall or a run already closes gets no panel. The planner builds this (kitchen_plan.house_cold), and the 3D model and the sheets draw the two new pieces from the catalogue.

Waiting on others

Lowering A's living-room sill, if the owner prefers the low view, would change the permit drawing. Nothing built here needs an engineer.
