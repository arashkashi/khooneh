73. Level showers and honest drains

21 November 2026

The owner asked for 1405 to be a design "relevant for generations to come", and for the work to follow the roadmap the generations review wrote. Its first round joins two plans that both reach into the wet rooms: a level shower in every home, and drains that are honest about the floor they run in. The owner had handed such decisions to the architect, so the architect took them, and left each one open for the owner to change.

A shower you can walk into, in every home

People who grow old in their own home usually stop managing at the shower: the step over a tray's edge is where they fall, and where a helper can't follow. Until now only the grandparents' home on the first floor was built for that. The rule that asks for a stand-up shower beside every tub now also asks each home on a list for at least one level shower, with no edge at all, at least 0.9 by 0.9 m. The list, and the size, are the owner's numbers to change. The architect put all four homes on it.

Three showers changed, each in exactly the place and size it had: the caretaker's bath, the small duplex's shower room and the big family's bath on the top floor. Their floors fall to a drain instead of standing in a tray. No wall moved, no room grew or shrank. The caretaker's bath sits on the floating floor over the grandparents' home, which keeps their footsteps quiet; its drain passes through that floor in a sealed collar that doesn't touch it, so the floor still floats.

The big family's only level shower is in the couple's bath, which opens from their bedroom. A second switch, off for now, would ask for one off the hall. Then the children's bath on the floor below would have to become level too. Its slab is already lowered for its drains, so that change is cheaper now than it was.

Drains under the floor, not in it

The gaps study found that the rule for drains let every pipe and its fall use the whole 140 mm of floor under the tiles. But the same 140 mm holds the floating screed that keeps footsteps from the home below, or a heated screed. A pipe can't run inside a floating screed: it would join it to the slab and carry the footsteps through. Told the truth, about 70 mm left for the drains, ten wet rooms were short by 3 to 61 mm, and the small duplex's dishwasher could only reach a riser under another home's floor.

The rule now takes the screed off: the drains get the floor's depth less the screed, 70 mm, a number the owner can set between 50 and 100 mm. The cure the study tested is built: under each short room the top of the slab steps down by what that room lacks, from 10 mm under the small duplex's linen store to 110 mm under the big family's kitchen. Eleven such steps, three of them deepened from yesterday's 60 mm. The tiles stay level; only the slab underneath is shaped. A deeper floor everywhere was the other cure, and it would have pushed nine yard windows into the beams and lowered the car park's ceiling under the grandparents' bath. The local steps break no rule: the rooms below them keep their height.

The eleven steps go on the structural engineer's list, with the two that were already there and stay as they are (under the grandparents' guest WC, and the big family's bath on the old terrace): the slab is 0.20 m and each step is a recess in it. No home gains or loses any floor.
