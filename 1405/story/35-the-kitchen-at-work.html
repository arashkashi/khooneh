The story

35. The kitchen at work

11 November 2026

You said on 11 November: "kitchen is an important place, and we should be able to numerically optimize where everything in kitchen is located and how they are related to other items", and you named the moments that matter: preparing the food, arriving home with groceries, cleaning up the dishes, the way from the hob to the table, the tea always ready, knives and the boiler on the counter, professional sinks, open and closed shelves, and a table for the four or five who live there rather than the table for eight.

Who eats at the table

The project counts the people who sleep in each home: A 3, B 4, C 3, D 2. The kitchen now keeps a second number, the everyday diners: by default the sleepers, but at least 4 and at most 6. It sizes the everyday table and the kitchen's walks; the rooms keep the sleepers until you answer the card wip-kitchen-households.

Everything in the kitchen, as numbers

  • An inventory of 128 things (the samovar, the knives, the polo pot, rice by the season, bread in the freezer, the detergents) each with its size, how often it is used, the height it should be kept at and the work centre it belongs to. It is now source data that the pipeline reads.
  • A planner for every kitchen (a new step before the furniture search). It takes each kitchen's runs as drawn and searches where the sink, its drainer, the hob, the oven, the dishwasher slot, the tea station and the landings go: first on a 15 cm grid, then on a 5 cm one. Hard rules are never broken; every other fault and every metre walked has a price. A deep single bowl with a drainer and a pull-down spray tap is the default sink, because fewer than one Iranian home in ten has a dishwasher.
  • Cupboards, drawers and shelves. Drawers at the prep worktop and the hob; the bins and the latched detergents under the sink; open shelves only for the daily glasses and never near the hob; a tall larder where a run meets a wall. Each of the 128 things is given a place: daily things between 0.6 and 1.6 m, nothing heavy above 1 m, knives on a strip at the prep worktop. What doesn't fit goes to the home's store, or to its share of the building's store in the basement.
  • Fourteen scenarios walked on the plan: morning tea, a polo dinner, the weekly shopping, the daily bread, the washing-up by hand, guests, two people cooking at once, the herbs, the rice bought by the season. Each is scored for the metres walked, the load carried, the times it cuts through the cook's working strip, the hands full at a door, things out of reach, and two people in each other's way.

The everyday table

Every home now has a table for six beside its kitchen, 1.6 x 0.9 m, which opens to 2.4 m for eight guests. In A and B it takes the place of the living-room table for eight; in C it stands in the hall by the kitchen, and the living room keeps its table for six. The hall tables against a wall now seat four, two of them at the ends. On the drawings the tables carry plates and cutlery instead of a fruit bowl and a jug, which had read as a hob and a sink, and the floor sheets no longer draw the beds and the floor cushions on top of each other.

What it found

  • The weekly walking and carrying score fell in A (from about 3,250 to 2,290) and B (2,950 to 2,690), and a little in C (4,560 to 4,360). D had no table before, so its new score (about 3,980) counts walks that didn't exist.
  • C's and D's kitchens hold too little: about twenty things in C and a dozen in D, some of them used every day, find no cupboard and would be fetched from the store; the rule KZ-fits names them, and that is what keeps C's score high. C's and D's prep worktops are short of 1.2 m. B's tea station is 8 m from its living seats.
  • Some work-triangle legs grew a little: the planner shortens the walks people really make instead.

All of it is on the design page under The kitchen at work, and on a 1:20 kitchen sheet per home in the drawings.
