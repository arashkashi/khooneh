62. What the sofa faces

20 November 2026

The small duplex's sofa stood on its only solid wall and looked at the end of the dining table, 1.1 metres away. "It is just strange to me," the owner wrote, and asked what was happening there, and in every living room of the building. The study that followed (research/living-room-1405.md) found why: the furniture search priced a sofa only by its relation to a television. Where there was no wall for one, every place scored the same, and "back to the wall" won. Of the variants it drew, one fixed the story simply: a two-seat sofa turned to face the yard window and the balcony door, with a low table in front of it. The owner chose it: "let us go with this B. D1: 2-seat sofa facing the yard, with a low table", and then "switch on the three rules".

Three rules that look where the seat looks

The search now knows what a seat faces. A sofa must not look at the backs of dining chairs or at a table's end close in front of it. It must face something: a television, a window or glazed door filling a fifth of its view, or another seat. And the household with one guest should be able to sit as one group, with two seats facing or meeting at a corner. The first two are now part of the price the search pays for an arrangement. The third is reported only: it would ask the caretaker's home for an armchair the owner has not approved.

A low table that can stand where it belongs

The study had found two rules that could never both pass. One asked for a coffee table 40 to 50 centimetres in front of the sofa; the other forbade anything in the sofa's 60 centimetres of floor in front. Now the paired table may stand there, and nothing else may. The search can finally place one, and every living room's list now asks for one where it keeps the ways. The caretaker's home has one. The step-free home does not: its only place, between the sofa and the television, would leave 83 centimetres where the ways from the balcony door run.

The spiral stair, checked

D1 put the sofa's back to the spiral stair. The study's caveat was whether anyone walks behind it. The stair's first tread starts on its east side, toward the dining bay, and its rail runs over every tread from the foot. So the living room's side of the stair is a rail, not a way. The rules now read it so: a spiral's side away from its foot is no way in, and a sofa may back onto it.

What the measurement then found

With the stair read correctly, the search was asked to find D1 itself, and it would not. Measured on a five-centimetre grid, with the sofa in D1's place, no place for the four-seat table leaves a way from the entry and the dining bay into the living room. The room is 3.55 metres across. The sofa takes 1.6 of it along the stair, and the table with its chairs takes the rest but 0.4 metres. The only other way in would run through the kitchen, past the hob. The study had counted these as a few extra hits; it had not tested whether the room still joined up. The owner's oldest standing rule is that furniture never narrows the way.

So the two-seat sofa is built, aimed by the search: on the west wall in the yard corner, facing the balcony door and the light, with the table turned along the stair and its four chairs, and the room still joined to its entry. Its seat no longer looks at a chair's back or a table's end; the study's score for the room rose from 51 to 71 (D1 as drawn: 85). The television is gone from both ways of living there, as D1 meant, and no low table fits without cutting a way. What D1 itself needs, a smaller table for two that opens to four, or a way through the kitchen, or the banquette of D3, is the owner's to choose. Nothing in the floor changed in any home.
