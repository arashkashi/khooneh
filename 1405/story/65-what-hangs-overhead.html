The story

65. What hangs overhead

21 November 2026

The owner looked at the caretaker's bath on the design page and asked: "what happens here in the house keepers bathroom on the bath tub? there seem to be an issue fix it with an additional rules please". There were two problems, and both came from the round that gave every home a place for its washing. The winter airer, a pulley rack of six bars at 2.1 m, had been hung over the caretaker's tub, wholly on its far side against the wall. To load it you would lean 0.9 m over a wet tub with an armful of washing. And the plan drew it as a solid piece standing on the tub, so the tub disappeared. The shelf over each washing machine did the same to the washer and its letters.

Drawn above the cut

A plan is a cut through the building at about a metre's height. What is lower is drawn solid, what is higher is drawn dashed over it. The airer, the shelves over the washers and the coat rails by the front doors were drawn at floor level because nothing told the drawing how high they hang. Now every piece mounted from 1.2 m up is lifted to its height, as the 3D model lifts it, and drawn dashed with no fill, after everything that stands on the floor. The tub, the washer and the shoe cabinet show through, and hovering the dashed outline names the piece and its height: "ceiling airer, 2.1 m up". The design page, the floor sheets and the decision pages draw it the same way.

A rule for what hangs overhead

The new hard rule, ES-overhead, judges every piece mounted overhead in every home. Its underside keeps 2.0 m of headroom over floor people stand on; over a washer or a shoe cabinet nobody stands, so a shelf at 1.2 m is fine there. It keeps clear of every shower's spray. It can be lowered to 1.2 m to load, with floor to stand on within reach of every bar and nothing under it in the way down, and it never asks for a lean of more than 0.4 m over a tub. With 0.6 m of washing hanging under it, it never meets a door's leaf, a window's opening sash or a mirror cabinet's doors. It stays off the ceiling lights and the extract fan, which are read where the 3D model puts them. All nine numbers are in the owner's rules file.

Measured on the places of the day before, three of the four airers failed. The caretaker's was over the tub. The grandparents' hung straight under a downlight and on the extract fan. The small duplex's was in the shower's spray, in front of the mirror cabinet's doors and almost under the light. Only the big home's passed. Every shelf and coat rail passed.

Where they hang now

The planner now hangs an airer only where the rule passes. The caretaker's moves off the tub, over the floor in front of it. The grandparents' bath is tight: two downlights, the fan, the shower and the tub leave no place clear of all of them, so the architect allowed an airer to reach a little over a tub as long as nobody leans more than 0.4 m to load it. The grandparents' now runs north to south between the shower and the tub, 0.15 m over the tub's rim, where it drips into the tub. The small duplex's bath is too small for a 1.8 m airer anywhere, so it gets a 1.5 m one along the basin wall. The big home's stays where it was. No floor changed in any home: the airers hang above the heads.

Two choices are left open for the owner. One is whether an airer may reach over a tub at all; without that, the grandparents' winter drying would leave their bath for the living room, too far from the washer. The other is how strict the rule should be: headroom measured to the bars rather than the washing, the shorter airer, and how far to keep off the lights.
