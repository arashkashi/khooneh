Chapter 17 · 7 November 2026

The site speaks

Iteration 3 had barely landed when the owner sent a new stack. The big family home becomes a duplex on the top two floors, F4 and part of F3. A smaller duplex of about 70 m² faces the yard on F3 and F2, for a family member or their guests. The caretaker takes the north side of F2. F1 is a normal one-level home. The basement, −1, holds no home: it becomes a shared room for gatherings and perhaps a gym. A small −2 returns, for storage and the plant room. The student flat leaves the programme, so five homes become four.

Before drawing, a small question about the front door: should it swing out to the corridor, where fewer people stand, or in, as most homes do? The answer became rule D8. Front doors swing into the home and the street door into the building. The landing and the pavement are shared and are the way out in a fire, so a leaf swung out there hits neighbours and narrows the escape. The cost is paid inside: each entry must be deep enough to take shoes off with the door open.

Decision. The new stack is the brief for iteration 4. It reverses earlier choices, which are now rechecked openly rather than quietly loosened: −2 had been dropped, the gym ruled out, and private stairs capped at one. The gym is for residents only, and the engineer prices −2 before we commit.

Then the site spoke. Until now the sun studies had leaned on guesses. The owner sent a map with north at the top. Measured on it, the street side faces 37° east of north, with the alley on the north-east and the yard to the south-west. The owner confirmed the rest. The building behind stands right on our rear plot line, with our yard between. The neighbours are standard four-floor Qazvin buildings like ours, the alley is 8 m wide, and the plot is about 200 m².

A new sun and daylight tool, built to be reused on every design, gave the verdict for iteration 3. On 21 December the top floor's yard side gets about seven hours of sun. F3 gets one to three. F1, F2, the basement and the sunken court get almost none, and the street side gets half an hour at most. The court floor sees about an hour and a half of sun even in June.

What it means. The sun lives at the top of this building. The new stack happens to fit: the big family home takes the sunny floors, and the basement becomes shared space rather than someone's home. The court can't be a sunlit garden at its floor. Its tree has to climb to the light. The owner would like a big tree and named four poplars. Their roots, suckers and short lives sit badly beside basement walls, so the court study is weighing them against a Persian ironwood, a hackberry and the plane tree.

Later the same day, the court study came back, and it was honest. With the rear building on our line, the court floor gets no sun in December and about an hour and a half in June, whatever shape it takes. The glass of the basement sees barely 1.5% of the sky. Shape matters less than colour: light walls do more than any widening. So the court is designed as a cool, shaded room open to the sky and a view from the lift, not a sunny garden. It is 7.55 × 5.5 m clear on natural ground, with a two-flight stair down from the bridge on the east, an all-glass front with a pair of hinged glass doors, beds along the walls, and a raised stone basin instead of a pool through the glass.

Decision, proposed. One tall, narrow tree in a deep soil pit. The tree's height decides its sun, not its place: a crown at 6 m sees under an hour of December sun, one at 18 m more than six. The poplars are not recommended: their roots reach many times the 3.5 m we have to the basement wall. First choice is a columnar hornbeam, second a male columnar ginkgo, with Persian ironwood as the smaller, safest option. The owner first chose the Persian ironwood and asked about a walnut, which needs full sun, spreads wider than the court and holds back the plants around it. Then they settled on what they had pictured all along: a silver birch, tall, quick, straight up, with a white trunk. Its crown can race up to the light, and its roots are far gentler than a poplar's. The price is regular water in Qazvin's dry summers and a shorter life. Of its two forms, the owner took the upright one: it fits the court without crowding the balconies, though its crown will stay below the sunny zone. If it struggles in its first years, a hornbeam or ironwood takes its place.

Two more tools joined the bench. The two finished earlier designs, 1399 and 1403, were built as 3D models in Blender straight from the plan data, floor by floor. The models showed what the data had left unsaid: door heights, a loft read by eye, doorways with no wall around them. And the owner asked for optimisation. The agreed use is narrow: it tunes a plan that already works, never invents one. It searches a few dimensions, such as the court's size, the tree's place and the depth of balconies, and shows a handful of best compromises for the owner to choose from.

Along the way the design folder was tidied. Superseded notes and the parked options moved into the story's archive. Each stage of the work now has a version tag, from v0.1.0 to v0.6.0.
