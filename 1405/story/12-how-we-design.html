How we design

A process had been forming without a name: questions first, then numbers, then checks, with a critic reading each draft. The owner asked for it to be named and introduced. The AI surveyed the established ways of designing a building, from British and American professional stages to Iranian practice, Christopher Alexander's participation, cohousing groups, design thinking and computational methods, and was asked to pick one.

The choice. The backbone is the RIBA Plan of Work 2020, a British map of eight stages. It says what each stage must produce and who signs it off, and leaves the rest of who does what to each project. That suits a project where an owner and an AI do the early work, and an engineer and an architect come later.

The two earlier attempts with an architect had left three lessons. A written brief isn't enough, and wishes about quality need a shared vocabulary early. Who decides what, how often to review and when details get fixed should be agreed before anyone draws. And market against personal priorities should be decided openly. The owner has settled that last one: this is a family building. The Plan of Work has a named place for the other two.

The stages

The Persian labels are the nearest Iranian steps, a rough match that local practice still has to confirm. Iranian law sets its own gates on top. Municipalities accept only drawings signed by licensed designers, and an appointed supervisor reports as the building rises. So the owner and the AI can't produce the permit drawings themselves, whatever the checks say.

What surprised us. On this map, the drawing the AI is making now isn't the design. Stage 1 “is not a design stage”, and a drawing made in it tests whether the brief fits the plot. So the first version from the owner's answers is a test, and the concept stays open for the people who join at Stage 2.

Stage 1 isn't finished. The contradictions in the answers now have researched answers for the owner to confirm or overturn, 16 questions only the owner can answer are still open, the plot isn't chosen, and the municipality hasn't confirmed the limits on floors and height.

Who decides

  • The owner holds the brief and signs each stage. Rejecting a proposal takes a written reason, or a changed brief.
  • The engineer can stop anything on structure or ground.
  • The architect proposes the concept freely within the brief, and each idea gets a fair test before it is dropped.
  • The AI drafts, checks, researches and criticises. When answers contradict, it researches and writes a new answer, as the owner asked, and the owner can overturn it. Otherwise it decides nothing.
  • People answering the form give evidence, without a vote. The neighbours' buildings are facts to survey.

When details get fixed

The rule has two halves: fix what is needed early, and how it is met as late as is responsible. The tree is essential, and that is settled in the brief. Its soil and its pit against the foundation get a reserved size at the concept stage, with the engineer, because they can't wait. Its species can wait for the working drawings. A court wide enough for the caretaker's rooms to keep daylight works the same way, and so do ceiling heights and column lines.

A comment finer than the current stage isn't refused and doesn't reopen the whole. It goes on a list with the stage at which it must be settled. Once a stage is signed, it reopens only through a recorded change.

The weak spot

The Plan of Work assumes a professional lead designer, and for now there isn't one. Checks catch failures, not good architecture, so a tidy computed draft could harden into a concept nobody with design judgement chose. That is why AI drawings stay labelled as tests, why an engineer is asked for a first view of the risks during Stage 1, why the engineer and the architect join fully at the start of Stage 2, and why people, not scripts, judge how it feels.
