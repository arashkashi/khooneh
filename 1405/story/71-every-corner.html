The story

71. Every corner

21 November 2026

The owner asked for something different from a new room or a new rule: "I want you to have a background agent for review, investigate each corner, visualize it for yourself and see what could go wrong." A reviewer went through all 158 rooms on eight levels, swept every door and every window sash, stood in front of every riser, looked at the floor sheets and the walk pictures, and came back with 21 findings. Ten of them mattered. The owner's method is that a real finding becomes a general rule with its numbers in the rules file, tested at both ends of its range, and that the design is then fixed by the planners, not by hand. The architect was asked to decide the open questions. This chapter is that round.

A child at an open window

On the big home's children's floor, each desk stands under a street window whose casement opens inward from 0.9 m: 0.15 m above the desk top, 9.8 m above the street, with the chair as the step up. The old guard rule only measured the sill from the floor. The new hard rule, WIN-climb, looks for any desk, bed or sofa a child can climb within half a metre of an opening casement, in a home where a child lives. It found four windows: the two children's rooms, the homework corner, and the parents' room upstairs, where the two-year-old sleeps beside a desk under the window. Each casement now has a child-safety restrictor that holds it to 100 mm until an adult releases it; the small hopper above still airs the room at night. The sheets mark them R100.

Where the heat and the cool come out

The services had said "low radiators under the windows". But beds, desks, sofas and kitchen counters stand under most windows, and the halls and baths have none. Measured by the new rule EM-place, not one heated room of the four homes had a placed emitter. The answer is floor heating, laid under the screed that is already in the floor, in every room but the baths, never under fitted cupboards. The floor was checked first, as one build-up: the heating's 2.5 cm panel and 4.5 cm screed are the 7 cm layer the floor already keeps above the drains, so the drains, their falls and the slab's recesses under the wet rooms stay exactly as the drains round left them. The kitchens are so full of counters and tall cupboards that floor heating alone falls short, so each gets a small fan heater in its plinth. Every bath and WC gets a towel radiator on a wall the rule found free. Under the grandparents' floor, over the car park, the heating needs more insulation than the panel gives, so the car park's ceiling is lined with stone wool.

The cooling pipes reached every home and stopped. Four outdoor units stood on the stair-house roof with nothing inside to connect them to. The rule CL-place now asks for a unit in each home with a duct to every bedroom and living room. The architect chose a slim ducted unit in a hall's ceiling, one on each floor of each home, after measuring the headroom the card asked for: 2.57 m under the bulkhead, 2.42 m where a duct passes under a beam.

A plumber at the riser

The riser beside the lift carries every home's water, heating, cooling and electrics, and on the first floor its only hatch was behind the grandparents' level shower and basin. The riser in the caretaker's kitchen was behind the counter. The rule RS-hatch-front asks every riser, on every level, for 60 cm of free face and 70 cm to kneel on. Now each home's valves and heat meter sit in a small fire-rated cabinet on its landing, clear of the lift's door, so shutting off a home's water or reading its meter needs nobody to enter a home; a leak in the riser itself is still reached from inside one. The riser's hatch in the shower room is declared, and the furniture search keeps the floor in front of it clear, so the basin moved. In the caretaker's kitchen the plain cupboards in front of the riser get loose backs. The third riser's hatch had been labelled "landing" although the landing touches its face for 5 cm; hatches are now named by the room that owns the face, and any hatch or cabinet in the stair's walls must be fire-rated like its doors.

Water, a roof light and the boards

The sunken court drains to a single pump, and the doors from the shared floor onto it have 20 mm sills, kept low for the step-free way to the birch. A failed pump in a summer cloudburst would flood the shared room. The rule TH-flood asks for a high sill or a guarded pump; the court now has two pumps that take turns, an alarm to the caretaker and a battery. On the roof, the roof lights had rails on two sides, but the potting station now runs along the third. Its rail is drawn on the roof sheet and built in the roof's 3D details. Each home's electrical board, router and door phone now has a recess by its front door, clear of the door's leaf.

The drawings themselves

The ground-floor sheet had its plan drawn three times too large, running under the title block: a loop drawing the car gate's leaves had borrowed the name of the sheet's scale. The kitchen sheets had labels printed over one another and elevations over the schedule. The build now measures every sheet's title block, scale bar, north arrow, labels, elevations and schedule, and stops if any of them overlap.

Left as they are

Five bed heads stand under casements that open into them. The architect keeps them: the hopper above airs the room, and the casement opens wide only for cleaning. A soft rule, F8-sash, lists them. The big home's guest WC door faces its dining table, but the owner is having that floor redesigned, so a soft rule, P-wc-view, will judge the new door. The basin under the big home's bath window and the kitchen windows over the worktops wait for the rounds working on those rooms.
