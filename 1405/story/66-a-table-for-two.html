66. A table for two

21 November 2026

The study of the small duplex's living room measured four ways to keep the owner's D1, the two-seat sofa facing the yard. The owner chose the one that measured best: "D1 + drop-leaf". The couple eat at a table for two every day, and on a guest night the table is moved and opened for four. They accepted both of its conditions: the everyday table seats two instead of four, and the table may be moved before it is opened.

Built by the planners

Nothing was pinned in place. The table is a gateleg after a catalogue one: 26 cm folded, 89 cm with one leaf up, 152 cm with both, 80 cm wide. It is now in the furniture library; on the plans its folded leaf and hinge are drawn dashed, and hovering it says "drop-leaf table, 2 to 4". The kitchen planner gives the small duplex this table and stands it by the kitchen's open edge, with a chair on each long side. Then the furniture search, left free, put the sofa exactly where D1 drew it: its back on the spiral stair's rail, facing the yard, with the low table in front. The study's story score for the room is 90, up from 71. No wall had room for the table with its two chairs and the floor behind them, so it stands free.

The kitchen's aisle

The study had seen that the opened table reaches into the kitchen. The guest rule could not see it, because the kitchen has no door of its own, so no way in is cut. The rule now measures the kitchen's work aisle in front of its worktop and fridge, and asks for 0.9 m. Moving the table is allowed, so the search tries every place and takes the one that keeps the most aisle, not the first one that fits.

For the small duplex there is no good place. Wherever the table opens for four, its guests' chairs stand in the aisle. The best place leaves 0.23 m in front of the fridge. That shuts the kitchen while the guests are seated, and the rule says so. Whether that is acceptable on a guest night is the owner's question, card wip-d-guest-aisle. Until they answer, the guest rule fails for the small duplex, and a failed must-hold rule caps the kitchens' score: the overall index falls from 76.5 to 73.3. Accepting the narrow aisle on guest nights would put it at 76.7, the small duplex at 83.5. The other three homes open their tables as before, and their aisles are untouched.

Ways among the furniture

The study also found a gap in the connection rule. It read rooms, not furniture. A way that only got through by squeezing behind a cook at the hob still passed, although the rule's own words say the way never runs through a kitchen. Now the way through halls and living rooms is walked among the furniture that stands there, 0.75 m wide, and the kitchen is never part of it. Every floor of every home passes, the small duplex's new living room included. The four-seat table beside the sofa, the study's third option, would now fail.

No floor area changed in any home.

Cook first, then open the table

The same day the owner answered the aisle question: "2. A". On a guest night the cooking is done and the food set out before the table is opened, so the narrow kitchen aisle is accepted, for the small duplex and on guest nights only. It became one setting in the owner's rules file: the guest rule accepts 0.2 m for that home, and none for the others. Nothing was pinned. Given the setting, the kitchen planner stood the everyday table 0.45 m further north, at the dining bay's edge, a shorter walk from the hob (1.68 m to 1.45 m), and on a guest night carries it 0.86 m to the same place as before, which leaves 0.23 m in front of the fridge while the guests sit. The sofa and the low table stayed where they were. The guest rule now passes for the small duplex, the kitchens' score is no longer capped, and the overall index rose from 73.8 to 76.5, the small duplex from 79.7 to 84.8. No floor area changed.
