The story

80. A cupboard by the stair

21 November 2026

On the big family's living floor there is a narrow strip between their own stair and the west party wall, 0.7 m wide and 1.65 m long. Two days earlier the floor audit had found it walled in with no way in, and opened it as a nook of the family hall, marked "coat hooks and a shelf". Nothing was ever planned in it. The owner looked at it on the design page and asked: "add more details to this space please asses how this could be used again, review and if needed revise this space a little bit."

What the spot is

Measured from the plans, not guessed:

  • The clear strip is 0.65 × 1.6 m once the stair's balustrade is allowed for.
  • It is open to the family hall at its short end, and open to the stair well along its whole long side.
  • It has no window, but the roof lights over B's stair and hall cover 43% of it, so it is lit from above by day.
  • It is 9.9 m from the front door and 14 m from the kitchen, but only 1.9 m from the stair. That makes it the place in the home closest, on average, to all three bedrooms on both floors: 11.7 m against the pantry's 19.5.
  • No main way runs through it.

Who would use it

The family in this home (fictional, made up to test the plan) are two parents, a daughter of 15, a son of 11 and a toddler of 2 who "walks and climbs everything" and can't be left alone near a stair. That decides a lot. A bench, a seat, a desk or open shelves in a strip 0.65 m wide always stand within half a metre of the stair's guard, so each becomes a step over it. Coats and shoes already have a bay at the front door that is only a quarter full. The mother's video calls need a room with a door, not a niche on the stair.

And the plan had a real gap. When the top floor was rebuilt that morning for a clear way home, the essentials planner moved the vacuum, the mop, the cleaners, the ironing board, the step ladder and the tools, about 200 litres, onto the walk-in pantry's shelf, beside 890 litres of food.

The choice

Seven uses were scored, 0 to 10, on five counts: does it fit, is it safe beside the stair, does it fill a real need, is it near where it is used, does it get the light it needs. The cleaning cupboard scored 9. Leaving the nook open scored 7. A plant and display niche scored 6, and so did a linen cupboard. The coat drop and the reading seat scored 4, the desk 3.

So the nook becomes the household's cleaning cupboard, built from the catalogue like every other piece:

  • a tall cupboard, 0.65 m wide and 0.6 m deep, on the nook's end wall, facing the hall;
  • two hinged doors with a child latch at 1.5 m. The owner prefers curtains on small stores, but cleaning liquids behind a curtain, with a two-year-old in the house, are a danger. That departure is deliberate and is on the card;
  • inside, a socket for the stick vacuum's charger, the mop and bucket, the broom, the ironing board and the ladder clipped upright, and the cleaners on the top shelves, out of a child's reach;
  • a light inside on a door switch.

The 0.9 m of floor in front stays in the nook, and nothing low stands by the stair's guard.

The pantry is now marked as a food store, so the planner gives its shelves to food only. The cleaning things go to the new cupboard by themselves. The guests' bedding that had been on the pantry shelf goes down to the home's cage. No floor moved: B's plan area is unchanged.

A rule from it

The nook was named and marked, yet nothing was ever planned in it. A new rule, LI-use, now asks that every room of a home that people use, of half a square metre or more, holds at least one planned piece. The size is tunable. It found the twin nook a floor below, beside the children's play hall, which is just as empty. It also found three smaller corners with nothing in them: the caretaker's hall by the kitchen, the small duplex's dining corner, and the north end of the play hall. The rule is soft, so it lowers the score a little (73.8 to 73.7) without blocking anything. Those four are left as a proposal for their own round.
